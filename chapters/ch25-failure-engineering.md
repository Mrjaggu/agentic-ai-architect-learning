# Chapter 25: How Agentic Systems Fail

> Seven incidents in this course so far — a $9k SMS bill, a refund paid twice, a policy waiver approved against a stale document, a fraud flag that outlived its own evidence by eight months, a clause cut in half, a phone call to someone who'd already left, a fallback model that quietly changed policy for fifty minutes — were each investigated as if they were the first of their kind. They weren't. They're seven instances of a small, nameable set of ways agentic systems fail. This chapter is the taxonomy that makes the eighth incident recognizable before it becomes a postmortem.

## 1. The taxonomy

"The model was wrong" is never the root cause of an agent incident, in the same way "the query was slow" was never the root cause of a database incident — it's the symptom that starts the investigation, not the finding that ends it. Every failure story cited above, read carefully, traces back past the model's output to a specific, nameable gap in the system around it: a missing rate limit (Ch5), a missing idempotency key (Ch6), a missing freshness check (Ch8), a missing expiry policy (Ch9), a chunk boundary that didn't match a clause boundary (Ch10), a delegation message missing one field (Ch15), a fallback path that never cleared the eval gate the primary did (Ch18). In every case the model reasoned coherently over what it was given — the failure lived in the scaffolding, not the reasoning, which is exactly Ch5's founding claim about harness design turned into a diagnostic habit: when an agent produces a wrong-but-fluent answer, the question isn't "why did the model think that" — it's "what did the system hand the model, or let the model do, that made that answer look right."

That reframing is what makes a taxonomy possible at all. If failures were genuinely as varied as the tasks agents perform, cataloging them would be futile — but they aren't. Every agent incident this course has covered so far sorts cleanly into one of five categories, each with its own characteristic shape and its own family of mitigations:

- **Reasoning failures** — the agent's own decision-making goes wrong even though every tool and every document it touched was healthy: picking the wrong tool for the job, looping past the point where it should have stopped.
- **Tool failures** — the agent's decision was fine, but an action it took (or a result it received) failed, timed out, half-completed, or lied.
- **Context/memory failures** — what entered the window or the long-term store was stale, mis-shaped, or simply too much, and the model reasoned correctly over bad material.
- **Coordination failures** — specific to multi-agent and ensemble topologies: two agents disagree, or one agent's defect propagates through a pipeline instead of staying contained to its own step.
- **Infrastructure failures** — the layer underneath all of the above: a provider outage, a network partition, a dependency degrading — which doesn't fail an agent directly so much as it triggers whichever reliability mechanism (Ch18's circuit breakers and fallbacks) is supposed to absorb it, and that mechanism's own behavior becomes the thing that can go wrong.

Notice what the categories buy you that a flat list of ten failure modes doesn't: they tell you *where to look*. A reasoning failure is caught by evals and tool-design review, before the incident, not by better monitoring after. A tool failure is caught by the backend architecture (Ch6) and the harness (Ch5) — timeouts, idempotency, bounds. A context/memory failure is caught at assembly and write time (Ch8/Ch9), never by hoping the model notices staleness on its own. A coordination failure is caught by explicit resolution rules and bounded blast radius at every handoff (Ch15's discipline, extended in §3 and §4 below). An infrastructure failure is caught by the reliability machinery itself being held to the same governance as the primary path it's protecting (Ch18's hardest lesson). §3 below works through ten specific failure modes inside these five categories, citing the worked incidents you've already read wherever one exists, and building fresh material for the three modes that don't yet have a home anywhere in the course.

## 2. Why the industry needed it — the eighth incident that looked new

Every one of the seven incidents cited in this chapter's opening was investigated, fixed, and closed by a different team, reviewing a different chapter's worth of architecture, using a different vocabulary. The harness team called Ch5's incident a missing rate limit. The backend team called Ch6's incident a missing idempotency key. The knowledge team called Ch10's incident a chunking bug. Every fix was correct, locally. None of those teams' postmortems asked the question a taxonomy forces you to ask: *what else in our system shares this failure's shape, whether or not it has caused an incident yet?* A rate-limit gap on `send_notification` (Ch5) is the same failure mode — an unbounded loop meeting an unbounded side effect — as a missing per-call budget on any other side-effecting tool nobody has audited yet. Treating each incident as its own one-off is itself a failure mode, and it's the quietest one in this chapter, because it never shows up in an incident log; it shows up as the *ninth* incident, six months later, in a different agent, that a taxonomy would have flagged as "this is Ch5's failure mode, in a different tool" on day one instead of after a second $9,000 bill.

The industry reached the same conclusion from the research side. A 2025 UC Berkeley-led study, "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?" (Cemri et al., presented at NeurIPS 2025), built exactly this kind of taxonomy by hand-annotating over 1,600 execution traces across seven popular open-source multi-agent frameworks — and found that the failures clustered into a small, stable set of fourteen recurring modes, not a long tail of bespoke bugs. §7 below covers what that taxonomy found in more depth, but the finding that matters here is structural: once you have named categories, you stop discovering the same failure mode for the first time in every new agent you ship, and you start auditing existing agents against a checklist instead of waiting for each one to generate its own incident.

## 3. The catalog, worked

Ten failure modes, grouped by the categories from §1. For each: what it looks like, the mitigation pattern, and — where one exists — the chapter carrying the full worked story. Three of the ten (wrong tool selection, agent disagreement, cascading multi-agent failure) have no prior home in this course; they get original treatment here, and cascading multi-agent failure gets a full worked incident of its own in §4.

### Reasoning failures

**Wrong tool selection.** The agent has more than one tool that could plausibly apply to the task in front of it, and it calls the wrong one — not a malformed call (Ch13's schema validation catches that instantly), a *well-formed* call to the wrong capability, which means nothing in the harness flags it and the error is invisible until someone reviews the output. A dispute-investigation agent with both a fee-waiver policy tool and a fraud-policy tool can route a dispute with fraud undertones through the ordinary fee-waiver check purely because the two tool descriptions overlap more than their intended use cases do — and the agent then produces a coherent, confidently wrong-track recommendation. **Mitigation pattern:** treat tool selection as its own eval dimension (Ch17) — a labeled "which tool should fire here" test set scored independently of final-answer correctness, because a lucky guess on a easy case hides a real selection bug on a hard one; sharpen tool names and descriptions so near-duplicate tools are actually distinguishable (Ch13's tool-schema-as-API-design argument, applied specifically to disambiguation); and where two tools are genuinely close in applicability, insert an explicit disambiguation step — a cheap classifier or a menu — rather than trusting the primary model's judgment call alone under load.

**Infinite loops.** A node that should terminate keeps re-firing: an ambiguous state transition, a retry with no backoff ceiling, a reflection step that never converges on "done." Ch5's notification storm is this mode's canonical incident — an ambiguous state transition looped the `validate` node back onto an already-resolved dispute, and because the notification tool carried no rate limit, every pass fired a fresh SMS, 41,000 messages and a $9,000 bill before anyone noticed. **Mitigation pattern:** bound the loop structurally rather than hoping the model stops — `max_iterations` in the harness (Ch5), a rate limit on any side-effecting tool *regardless* of how a loop got triggered (the defense-in-depth lesson: fix the loop *and* cap what looping can do, because you won't catch every loop bug before it ships), and a convergence metric in evals and traces (Ch16/Ch17) that flags a run re-visiting the same node.

### Tool failures

**Tool timeout.** A tool call — or the model call wrapping it — runs longer than the caller is willing to wait; the caller gives up and retries; if the original call is still executing and the action isn't idempotent, the retry becomes a second real-world action. Ch6's duplicate-refund incident is this exactly: `check_policy`'s occasional 90-second runs outlived the mobile app's 60-second client timeout, the app silently retried the identical request, and the customer was refunded ₹4,300 twice, eleven days before anyone in reconciliation noticed. **Mitigation pattern:** idempotency keys at the submission boundary (Ch6) so a retried request returns the original job instead of starting a second one; typed timeouts per call, per tool, per run (Ch18) so a slow step fails predictably instead of outliving every caller's patience; and design for the *gap* between your own execution time and a caller's timeout — assume they disagree, because eventually they will.

**Partial failure.** A multi-step run dies partway through — step 9 of 10 — and without checkpointing, "partial" means "gone": work already completed (transactions fetched, policy already checked) is silently discarded, either lost outright or redone from zero with a real chance of landing on a different, inconsistent outcome the second time. Ch6 names this directly as one of the six ways a naive synchronous agent endpoint fails production. **Mitigation pattern:** checkpoint state after every node (Ch4/Ch6) so a crash resumes at the last completed step rather than from zero; make every step's side effect idempotent so a resumed run can safely re-attempt the step it was mid-way through without duplicating the step it had already finished; and make "resume, not restart" a backend design decision taken once, not an improvisation invented fresh during each incident.

**Hallucinated tool output.** Not the model hallucinating text — a *tool* returning a plausible but wrong result: a stub left wired into a production path, a tool that swallows an internal error and silently returns a cached or default value instead of raising, or a downstream API that is itself confidently wrong with no way for the agent to sanity-check it. The agent then reasons correctly over a lie it was handed, and produces a fluent, wrong output — structurally the same shape as Ch8's context-poisoning story, one step earlier in the pipeline: the poison enters through a tool call instead of a retrieval. **Mitigation pattern:** validate tool results at the boundary the same way Ch13 validates model output — a fetch that silently returns nothing when it should return five transactions is a signal, not a null to shrug past; instrument tool calls (Ch16) with result-shape and result-size telemetry so a suspicious pattern (every call returning the same suspiciously round number) becomes visible in aggregate even when no single call looks wrong in isolation; and never let a tool fail silently into a default value — fail closed (Ch5) and surface the gap instead of papering over it.

### Context & memory failures

**Context overflow.** The window fills with more than it needs — raw tool results, full conversation history, every retrieved chunk instead of the top few — and the model's reasoning degrades over the resulting mush well before it ever hits a hard token limit. Ch8 names the mechanism precisely: "lost in the middle" is a real, recurring property of attention over long context, not a training artifact newer model generations simply grow out of. **Mitigation pattern:** Ch8's assembly discipline in full — compression (raw results become stats plus a few samples, not the whole payload), pruning (drop what's superseded or resolved), and per-section token budgets enforced by the harness — so overflow is caught by policy before the model ever has to cope with the mush.

**Bad retrieval.** The retriever returns something topically relevant and structurally wrong — the wrong slice of a real document, or a real-but-superseded version of it — and because the returned text reads fluently on its own, nothing about it looks broken. Two banking incidents wear this mode: Ch10's "the clause that got cut in half," where a fixed 512-token chunk boundary split a fee-waiver clause mid-sentence and the truncated half scored *higher* on similarity search than the complete clause would have; and Ch8's stale-policy incident, where an unrefreshed RAG index kept surfacing a fee-waiver policy three weeks out of date, and the model reasoned correctly over a document it had no way to know was stale. **Mitigation pattern:** chunk boundaries chosen to match the citable unit, not a fixed token count (Ch10); freshness as a first-class, visible field on every retrieved chunk, with a pruning rule that actually acts on it (Ch8); and retrieval evals (Ch17) scored against a labeled set, because a retriever that "usually returns something relevant" can still be systematically wrong in exactly the way that matters most.

**Stale memory.** A long-term memory write outlives the evidence that justified it, and nothing in the system is designed to expire or reconsider it — so an inference that was wrong, or was true once and no longer is, keeps steering every future interaction as if it were current fact. Ch9's fraud flag is the incident: an unattributed, unconfirmed "fraud-risk transaction pattern" inference, written with no expiry and no link back to any actual case, quietly shaped fourteen separate interactions across eight months before a customer's ombudsman complaint finally traced the pattern of friction back to it. **Mitigation pattern:** remember by policy, not by default — an explicit allowlist of what may be stored (Ch9); provenance and confidence attached to every memory write so it can be audited and challenged; and expiry as a first-class feature, with `ttl_days` on anything risk-adjacent measured in days or weeks, never "forever" by silent default.

### Coordination failures

**Agent disagreement.** In any topology where more than one agent, model, or ensemble member independently produces a judgment on the same question, the outputs sometimes conflict — one fraud classifier flags a transaction, a second scores it clean; two specialized agents recommend opposite outcomes for the same fee-waiver dispute. Unlike a single agent's wrong answer, there's no "the model was wrong" to point at — both outputs can be individually well-formed, and the disagreement itself is the signal that needs resolving, not a nuisance to suppress by arbitrarily picking one. **Mitigation pattern:** never let a silent tie-break (first-response-wins, alphabetical-agent-wins, whichever finished faster) resolve a genuine disagreement — design an explicit resolution strategy per decision class instead: a third arbitration pass that is shown the disagreement itself, not just the two answers, so it can reason about *why* they diverge; a confidence-weighted vote where each agent's historical reliability on this specific decision type is tracked from evals (Ch17), so the vote isn't a coin flip dressed up as consensus; and, for anything customer-facing or irreversible, route the disagreement itself into human-in-the-loop (Ch20) rather than resolving it silently — a disagreement between two agents is exactly the kind of "not confident" signal Ch20 argues should escalate, not get smoothed into a single answer nobody actually agreed on.

**Cascading multi-agent failure.** Distinct from Ch15's join problem — which was a *miscommunication* in a single handoff, a bare name string missing a role field, producing one wrong outcome for one contact — a cascading failure is a *data-quality or reasoning defect in one agent's output that a downstream agent treats as ground truth and acts on, amplifying rather than catching it*, propagating through a pipeline at scale rather than derailing one request. Because each agent's own step looks locally correct — sound arithmetic performed on a corrupted input, sound policy logic applied to a wrong risk score — no single span in the trace looks broken, which is exactly what makes this mode dangerous: it's invisible at the level every other failure mode gets caught at, and only visible in aggregate. §4 below is a full worked incident. **Mitigation pattern:** never let a downstream agent trust an upstream agent's output as fact without a bounds check at the handoff — a sanity range, an anomaly threshold — the same discipline Ch13 applies to tool results, applied one level up at the agent-to-agent boundary; instrument pipeline-level metrics, not only per-agent ones, because a spike in *how many* downstream decisions look unusual in aggregate catches what no single trace ever will; and size the blast radius of any autonomous batch step to a volume a human can actually absorb reviewing, so a defect that reaches the pipeline surfaces in tens of cases, not thousands, before the next stage compounds it further.

## 4. A failure story — the overnight batch that graded its own homework

The card-services team's overnight credit-line review — the batch cousin of Ch21's interactive card-limit agent, generating next-morning recommendations for relationship managers to approve rather than acting on a single live request — ran as a three-stage pipeline across roughly 18,000 accounts a night. Agent 1, the Spend Pattern Analyzer, computed a rolling 90-day average spend and volatility score per account. Agent 2, the Risk Adjustor, combined that score with bureau data into a recommended limit change. Agent 3, the Recommendation Synthesizer, packaged each change into the relationship manager's morning queue with a per-claim citation — "recommend decrease: 90-day average spend down 42%" — exactly the citation discipline Ch21's reference flow expects.

A routine update to Agent 1 introduced a currency-parsing gap: transactions in a newly onboarded foreign merchant category, when the currency code didn't match the analyzer's allowlist, were silently scored as zero spend rather than raising an error. Roughly 1,100 of the 18,000 accounts — customers with regular international spend — had their 90-day averages understated as a result. Agent 2 held no bounds check on Agent 1's output; a sudden, large drop in average spend is exactly the signal its policy logic was built to act on, so it recommended limit decreases for 640 of those 1,100 accounts. Agent 3's citations were, individually, entirely accurate — the average *had* dropped 42%, by that night's flawed arithmetic — so nothing in any single recommendation looked wrong to a relationship manager scanning a normal-looking queue. The queue itself wasn't normal: it had swollen from a typical ~150 recommendations to over 900, but volume alone didn't trip any alert, and relationship managers approved that morning's decreases at close to their usual 79% rate, because each one, read on its own, was a coherent story. By early afternoon, 480 of the flawed decreases had been executed before a daily anomaly check — comparing total limit decreases against a 90-day rolling baseline — flagged that day's volume at roughly 4.5x normal and froze the remainder of the queue. A cluster of the 480 affected customers were traveling internationally that week; several had cards decline at point of sale before the bank's own outreach reached them. Remediation — reversing 480 limit changes and proactively contacting affected customers — took two days. No single agent's span, reviewed in isolation, ever looked broken: Agent 1's bug was silent, Agent 2's logic was sound given what it was handed, and Agent 3's citations were arithmetically correct. The failure was entirely in what propagated unchecked between them.

## 5. Design decisions

Six mitigation patterns recur across the catalog in §3; which one applies is a function of what actually broke, not a matter of taste.

- **Retry** — for transient, genuinely retriable failures only (Ch18's typed-retry taxonomy: retriable vs reroutable vs terminal), and only where the action being retried is idempotent (Ch6). Retrying a non-idempotent action without an idempotency key is precisely how Ch6's duplicate refund happened — the retry itself wasn't the bug, the *absence of a way to recognize it as a retry* was.
- **Circuit breaker** — for a dependency that is itself in a bad state (rising error rate on a model endpoint or tool), not for a model that returned wrong-but-well-formed content — that's a reroute or a terminal failure, not a breaker's job. A breaker protects your own latency and stops compounding a struggling dependency's problem; per Ch18's incident, whatever a breaker fails traffic over *to* inherits the same governance obligation as the path it's replacing, or the breaker becomes a policy change wearing a reliability costume.
- **Timeout** — the floor under every other pattern: no model call, no tool call, no node, no run without one (Ch5). A hang with no timeout doesn't stay contained as one failure mode; given enough time it becomes every other mode by starvation — the caller retries into it (tool timeout), the run dies holding partial state (partial failure), or a downstream agent waits on it and the whole pipeline stalls.
- **Fallback** — a deliberate, pre-evaluated alternate path, never an improvisation invented mid-incident. Ch18's fallback-that-changed-policy story is the cautionary worked example directly: the fallback model had cleared a schema-compliance check but never the same Ch17 eval suite the primary ran against, and it quietly approved a materially different band of fee waivers for fifty minutes with nobody watching for exactly that. A fallback earns the name only once it's been proven to make the same *class* of decision the primary makes, not merely to return the same *shape* of output.
- **Graceful degradation** — return a partial, honest result rather than force a hollow completion; this is the right answer to partial failure and context overflow specifically, where "here's what I found; I couldn't complete X" beats both silent failure and a fluent-but-fabricated finish.
- **Kill switch** — the last resort, for when the patterns above haven't contained a failure fast enough, or the blast radius already crossed an acceptable line: revoke the agent's identity (Ch19), halt at the platform level (Ch20). The one hard requirement is that halting checkpoints state cleanly — a kill switch that loses in-flight work isn't a control, it's a second incident stacked on the first.

Two of the ten catalog modes don't map onto this list at all, by design: wrong tool selection is caught by evals and tool-schema review *before* a run starts, not by a runtime pattern reacting after the fact, and agent disagreement is resolved by an explicit arbitration or escalation rule (§3), not by retrying, breaking, or falling back — there's no single "correct" output to retry toward. Cascading multi-agent failure is best handled by prevention — bounds checks at every handoff and batch-size limits on autonomous stages — because by the time it's visible enough to trigger a circuit breaker, as §4 shows, hundreds of downstream actions may already have executed.

## 6. Trade-offs

| Pattern | Blast-radius control | Latency / cost | Operator burden |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retry | Low — repeats the same action, so a non-idempotent retry *widens* blast radius (Ch6) | Cheap per attempt, but multiplies token cost silently if untyped (Ch18) | Low, until it isn't — an untyped retry policy is invisible until it's a bill |
| Circuit breaker | Good — stops new requests from reaching a failing dependency | Near-zero once tripped (fails fast); adds monitoring overhead to run | Medium — needs a tuned threshold and a recovery probe, or it flaps |
| Timeout | Bounds worst-case duration only, not correctness | Cheap to add, easy to under-tune (too short trips false positives, too long lets damage accumulate) | Low to set, but every value needs revisiting as real latency data comes in |
| Fallback | Only as good as the fallback's own governance — a fallback with no eval gate *widens* blast radius invisibly (Ch18's incident) | Usually the most expensive path at the moment you need it most | High — the fallback needs its own eval suite, kept current, or it's a liability wearing a safety net's name |
| Graceful degradation | Good — a partial result can't cause the harm a hallucinated complete one can | Cheap — it's a smaller output, not more computation | Low, but requires the UX and downstream systems to actually handle "partial" as a real state |
| Kill switch | Best possible — stops everything, everywhere, immediately | Zero marginal cost to trip; real cost is the halted work and the recovery afterward | Highest — needs a rehearsed drill, a clear owner, and a clean resume path, or the switch itself becomes the incident |

No single row in this table is sufficient on its own — Ch5's notification storm needed both a loop fix *and* a rate limit, because either one alone would have left the other half of the failure unaddressed. Reading this table as "pick one" is itself a design mistake; reading it as "which of these does this specific failure mode need, and does it need more than one" is the right use of it.

## 7. Industry implementation

The strongest recent grounding for treating agent failure as a taxonomy rather than a grab-bag of incidents comes from the research side. "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?" (Cemri, Pan, Yang, et al., presented at NeurIPS 2025) is exactly this chapter's exercise, done at scale: the authors hand-annotated over 1,600 execution traces across seven popular open-source multi-agent frameworks and built a taxonomy — MAST — of fourteen fine-grained failure modes grouped into three categories: specification and system-design issues, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification failures, validated with a high inter-annotator agreement score (kappa 0.88) that held up even when they automated the annotation with an LLM judge. The headline finding is the one worth taking into a design review: the most common failure mode wasn't a dramatic crash or a hallucinated fact — it was step repetition, agents redoing work they'd already completed, at nearly 16% of all coded failures, followed closely by a mismatch between an agent's stated reasoning and its actual action (about 13%) and agents simply failing to recognize that a task was already done (about 12%). Three unglamorous "the agent didn't know it was finished" failures outnumber anything resembling a classic software bug — which is this chapter's §1 argument arriving with a dataset behind it: a shared taxonomy, applied across many systems, finds failures cluster into a small, nameable set of recurring modes rather than a long tail of bespoke incidents each needing investigation from first principles.

Chaos engineering purpose-built for agents is the practical half of the same shift. Open fault-injection tooling for agents (the `agent-chaos`-style frameworks, paired with reliability benchmarks built specifically for agentic workloads) inject the same fault families this chapter's catalog names — model-layer faults like rate limits and truncated streams, tool-call faults like timeouts and malformed responses, and context degradation over long-running tasks — against a running agent, the same "attack your own system before an incident does" discipline Ch19's red-team lab applies to security, retargeted at reliability. One published benchmark run this way found a well-baselined agent's task success rate fall from the high nineties into the high eighties once realistic fault injection — timeouts, malformed tool data, simulated rate limiting — was layered on, and a further finding worth repeating to a cost-conscious CFO: model choice mattered far less to the resulting reliability score than the fault-handling architecture wrapped around it, with a dramatically cheaper model holding nearly the same reliability once both models sat behind the same fault-tolerant harness. That's Ch18's cost argument arriving from the reliability side of the ledger instead of the token-spend side.

The demand-side case for all of this is visible in current adoption data: industry surveys report a large majority of enterprises now running some form of agent in production with most planning further expansion, while analyst forecasts have flagged a meaningful share of agentic AI initiatives — Gartner's widely cited estimate is on the order of 40% by 2027 — as being at risk of cancellation specifically over inadequate risk controls and unclear governance, not model quality. Tracked AI-incident volume is rising year over year in the same period. Read together, the research and the adoption numbers say the same thing: the industry's own failure count is starting to outpace any individual team's ability to write a bespoke postmortem for each new incident, which is the exact pressure a taxonomy is built to relieve.

## 8. Hands-on lab

Build a failure-mode-to-mitigation matrix for the dispute-investigation agent (Ch2), then prove it by breaking the agent on purpose.

**Step 1 — the matrix.** For each of §3's ten modes, write one row: does this mode apply to the dispute-investigation agent's actual architecture (yes/no, and why); if yes, which mitigation pattern from §5 covers it; which existing chapter's control implements that mitigation in this agent (e.g., idempotency key at the job-submission API, Ch6); and how you'd detect the mode in production if the mitigation ever failed (which trace signal, which alert, from Ch16). Not every mode will apply — a single-agent dispute investigator has no agent-disagreement or cascading-failure exposure until it's decomposed into a pipeline, and that's a legitimate, useful finding to write down, not a gap in the exercise.

**Step 2 — inject three, on purpose.** Pick one mode from each of three different categories in §1 so the exercise covers more than one kind of failure: an infinite loop (force an ambiguous state transition to re-fire a node and confirm `max_iterations` and any side-effecting tool's rate limit both catch it independently — reproduce Ch5's storm on a toy scale); a tool timeout with no idempotency key first, then with one (reproduce Ch6's duplicate-refund bug, confirm it, then confirm the fix — the same two-stage discipline Ch6's own lab uses); and wrong tool selection (give the agent two overlapping tools on a borderline case and confirm your Ch17 tool-selection eval catches the misfire before it ever reaches production, not after).

**Step 3 — verify the catch, not just the trip.** For each injected failure, confirm two things separately: that the mitigation activated (the breaker tripped, the retry didn't duplicate, the eval flagged the selection), and that the activation is *visible* — in a trace, an alert, or an eval report — to someone who wasn't in the room when you injected it. A mitigation that fires silently is only half a mitigation; the other half is someone finding out.

Deliverable: the matrix from Step 1, annotated with pass/fail results from Step 2's three injections, in the same spirit as Ch19's catch/damage matrix — a small, honest table that says exactly what this agent is protected against today and what it still isn't.

## 9. Architect's take: the banking read

A risk committee that hears "we've had seven incidents and fixed all seven" has heard seven data points. A risk committee that hears "we maintain one taxonomy of ten failure modes, every incident maps to a named mode with a named mitigation and a named owner, and here's the matrix showing which of our production agents have been audited against all ten" has heard a control framework — the same shift Ch18's §9 argues for cost, applied here to reliability itself. The MRM inventory Ch20 already requires (every agent, its purpose, its owner, its autonomy level) extends naturally into this taxonomy: for every inventoried agent, which of the ten modes has been evaluated and mitigated, and which haven't yet, is a single additional column that turns "we think our agents are reliable" into a defensible, auditable claim. §4's cascading-failure story is the argument for why this can't stop at single-agent review: as pipelines and multi-agent topologies multiply across the bank's agent estate, the failure modes that matter most are increasingly the ones that only exist *between* agents, and a per-agent audit checklist alone will miss every one of them.

## Governance & security lens

A failure taxonomy is a governance artifact, not just an engineering one: it's what lets "how do you know your agents are reliable" get answered with a matrix instead of an anecdote. Every mitigation pattern in §5 carries its own governance weight — a fallback needs the same eval sign-off as the primary it replaces (Ch18), a kill switch needs a rehearsed drill and a named authority to pull it (Ch19/Ch20), and a circuit breaker's threshold is a decision with an owner, not a default an engineer picked once and forgot. Cascading multi-agent failure specifically raises the stakes on this: because no single agent's span looks broken in §4's incident, the audit trail that eventually explains what happened has to exist at the *pipeline* level, not just per-agent, or the postmortem stalls at "every component worked as designed" without ever finding the actual defect. Governing questions:

- For every production agent (and every multi-agent pipeline), which of this chapter's ten failure modes have been evaluated against, and which are still unaudited?
- When two agents disagree, or a batch pipeline's output looks anomalous in aggregate, is there a named human authority whose job is to see that signal — or does it only surface after enough individual cases accumulate to trip a threshold?
- Who is authorized to reset a tripped circuit breaker, re-enable a killed agent, or clear a paused batch queue, and is that authorization itself logged with the same rigor as the trip that preceded it?

## Interview Questions & Answers

**Q1: Why build a unified failure taxonomy instead of treating each production incident as its own investigation with its own fix?**

Because a one-off investigation only ever answers "what caused this incident," and never the more useful question, "what else in our system shares this failure's shape." Ch5's notification storm was fixed with one rate-limit line on one tool — correct, but it never prompted anyone to check whether any *other* side-effecting tool in the same agent had the same unbounded-loop-meets-unbounded-side-effect exposure, because the fix was filed as "a notification bug," not as an instance of a named, recurring failure mode. A taxonomy turns that audit into a checklist instead of a hope: once "infinite loop plus missing rate limit" is a named mode with a named mitigation, every tool in every agent can be checked against it directly. The research evidence backs this structurally — the MAST study found that across seven different multi-agent frameworks, failures cluster into the same small set of fourteen modes, which means the failure you haven't had yet almost certainly already has a name.

**Q2: What if two mitigations conflict — say, a circuit breaker trips during a legitimate traffic spike rather than a real dependency failure?**

This is exactly why §5 insists a circuit breaker's job is narrowly scoped to dependency health — it trips on error rate, not on request volume — and a spike in legitimate traffic that isn't accompanied by a rising error rate shouldn't trip it at all; if it does, the breaker's threshold is mistuned, not doing its job correctly. The actual conflict worth worrying about is different: a volume spike that *does* start degrading a dependency (slow responses turning into timeouts turning into errors) will legitimately trip the breaker, and the fix isn't to loosen the threshold — it's to ask whether the traffic spike itself needed a separate control, like a per-tenant rate limit or a queue, that would have kept the dependency healthy in the first place. Conflating "the breaker misfired" with "the breaker fired and something upstream should have prevented the condition that triggered it" is a common misdiagnosis, and the fix in the second case is upstream capacity management, not breaker tuning. The design habit worth stating out loud is that every mitigation pattern protects against one specific class of problem, and a spike that looks like a false positive is often actually two failure modes stacked on top of each other.

**Q3: After a kill switch fires on an agent, what actually happens next — how does the agent resume, and who is accountable for the decision to pull it?**

Firing the kill switch revokes the agent's identity (Ch19) and halts execution at the platform level (Ch20), and the one non-negotiable design requirement from §5 is that the halt checkpoints state cleanly — every in-flight run has to be resumable or safely abandonable, never left in an undefined partial state, or the kill switch itself becomes a second incident on top of whatever triggered it. Resumption is a deliberate, reviewed action, not automatic: someone with named authority — the agent's accountable human owner, per Ch19's non-human-identity model — has to confirm the underlying cause is actually fixed, not just that the alarm stopped firing, before the identity is reinstated and the agent's traffic resumes. Every kill and every resume is logged with the same audit rigor as any other production action, because "we pulled the switch" and "we put it back" are both facts a regulator or an internal MRM review will eventually ask to see, and the accountability question — who decided, on what evidence — has to have a clean answer both times, not just the first.

**Q4: What are the cost implications of over-defensive failure handling — for example, a retry policy that's too aggressive after a team reads a chapter like this one and overcorrects?**

This is the same trap Ch18 names directly: an untyped or overly generous retry policy is a cost leak wearing a reliability costume, because every retry re-sends the full context and re-runs the full model call at full token price, and a policy that retries generously "to be safe" can multiply a run's worst-case cost by however many attempts it allows without improving the actual success rate at all. The fix isn't fewer retries in the abstract — it's typed retries: retriable failures (timeouts, rate limits) get a bounded retry with backoff, reroutable failures (a schema mismatch) get exactly one repair pass with the validation error attached, and everything else is terminal and should fail fast and checkpoint, not loop. Reading this chapter as "add more defensive machinery everywhere" is itself a failure mode worth naming — the right response to a taxonomy is targeted coverage of the modes that actually apply to a given agent (§8's matrix exercise), not blanket retry-and-fallback logic layered onto every call regardless of whether the failure it's defending against is even possible there.

**Q5: What data security implications does a partial failure introduce — could a half-completed agent action leak customer state?**

Yes, in two distinct ways worth separating. First, an action that half-completes without checkpointing can leave a downstream system in an inconsistent state that itself constitutes a data-integrity exposure — imagine a step that updates a case record before a second step that's supposed to mask a sensitive field within it, and the run dies between the two; the intermediate state, sensitive field unmasked, may now be visible to whatever reads that record next. Second, and more subtly, a partial failure that gets retried without idempotency (§3's tool-timeout mode) doesn't just risk a duplicate action like Ch6's refund — it risks a duplicate write of customer data to two independent execution contexts, each with its own logs, its own trace, its own potential exposure surface, doubling the system's data footprint for a single customer event without anyone having decided that was necessary. The mitigation is the same architecture that fixes the reliability problem: checkpointing that captures state atomically at defined boundaries (never mid-mutation), idempotency keys that prevent the duplicate-write scenario outright, and audit logging (Ch16) detailed enough that a security review can reconstruct exactly what state existed, and where, at the moment any given run failed.

**Q6: Design the guardrail that would have caught §4's cascading multi-agent failure before it reached 480 executed limit decreases.**

The single highest-leverage guardrail is a bounds check at the Agent 1-to-Agent 2 handoff: a 90-day spend average shouldn't be allowed to drop by more than some sanity threshold (say, 30-40%) without the record being flagged for review rather than passed downstream as an ordinary input, because a legitimate spend collapse that large is rare enough to be worth a second look regardless of cause. Layered on top of that, Agent 3's synthesis stage should track pipeline-level statistics, not just per-recommendation correctness — a night where 640 of 18,000 accounts get decrease recommendations against a baseline of a few dozen is a signal on its own, independent of whether any single recommendation's citation is arithmetically correct. Finally, the batch's autonomous blast radius should be capped structurally: releasing recommendations to the RM queue in tranches capped at, say, 200 a night rather than all 900 at once would have meant the anomaly surfaced against the first tranche, not after 480 had already been approved and executed. None of these three guardrails requires catching the currency-parsing bug itself — the point of a cascading-failure guardrail is that it doesn't need to know what the specific defect was, only that the pipeline's aggregate behavior looks nothing like a normal night.

**Q7: How does access control apply to the mitigation machinery itself — who should be able to reset a tripped circuit breaker or re-enable a killed agent?**

The same least-privilege discipline Ch19 applies to tool grants applies here to the controls themselves: resetting a breaker or re-enabling a killed agent is a production action with real consequences, so it needs its own scoped permission, held by a named role — typically the agent's accountable owner plus an on-call reliability lead, not "any engineer with prod access." Critically, the person or system that *tripped* the control and the person who *resets* it don't have to be the same, but the reset itself has to be logged with the same delegation-record discipline Ch19 requires of every agent action — who reset it, on what evidence, at what time — because an unlogged reset is functionally the same governance gap as an unattributed memory write in Ch9's incident: an action with real consequences that nobody can later reconstruct or challenge. For anything that reached the L2 autonomy tier before it failed (Ch20's ladder), the reset decision arguably deserves the same review rigor as the original action would have — if a human had to approve the card-limit decrease, a human should approve turning the mechanism that produces those decreases back on.

**Q8: What deployment or production-readiness signals tell you a team's failure handling is actually mature, rather than just documented in a design doc?**

The clearest signal is whether the team can produce §8's kind of matrix — a live, current mapping from failure mode to mitigation to detection signal, for every agent in production, not a document written once at launch and never revisited as the agent's tools and topology changed. A second signal is whether failure injection actually happens before an incident forces it: a team that has run something like this chapter's chaos-engineering practice against its own agents, on purpose, on a recurring cadence (mirroring Ch19's red-team cadence), has real evidence its mitigations work, where a team that's only ever seen its circuit breakers and fallbacks activate during a real outage is running its first real test live, in production, with customers as the control group. A third signal, specific to multi-agent systems, is whether the team monitors pipeline-level aggregate metrics at all — §4's incident was invisible to every per-agent trace and only visible in the aggregate decrease-volume number, so an agent estate that only instruments individual agents, never pipelines as wholes, has a coverage gap it likely doesn't know about yet.

**Q9: Scenario — a bank runs an ensemble fraud-detection setup where two independently trained models each score every transaction, and on a specific transaction they disagree sharply: one flags it as fraud, the other scores it clean. Design the resolution.**

First, resist any temptation to resolve this with a silent default like "flag wins" or "average the two scores" — a sharp disagreement between two models that were each built to be independently reliable is informative on its own, and collapsing it into one number throws that information away. The right design routes disagreement by stakes: for a transaction where the dollar amount and customer profile put it below a defined risk threshold, a fast automated tie-break (weighted by each model's tracked historical precision on this transaction type, from Ch17-style evals segmented by scenario) is proportionate; for anything above that threshold, or where the two scores diverge by more than a defined margin, the disagreement itself routes to a human fraud analyst, framed explicitly as "two models disagree, here's what each saw" rather than a single blended verdict that hides the fact that a live disagreement even occurred. Over time, every resolved disagreement — human or automated — becomes an eval case that either model's future version has to answer for, which is how an ensemble's disagreement rate becomes a monitored production metric instead of a one-off event each time it happens. The underlying principle from §3 holds regardless of domain: a disagreement is a signal to escalate proportionate to stakes, never a coin flip dressed up as consensus.

**Q10: Scenario — you're reviewing a new multi-agent pipeline before it goes to production: three agents in sequence, each holding one tool, output of one feeding directly into the next. What do you specifically check for, given this chapter's taxonomy?**

I'd start with the handoffs, not the agents — per §4 and §3's cascading-failure entry, the failure that matters most in a pipeline like this rarely lives inside any single agent's reasoning, it lives in what one agent hands the next unchecked. Concretely: does every handoff carry a bounds or sanity check on the upstream output before the downstream agent treats it as fact, the way Ch13 already expects of tool results; is there a pipeline-level metric (not just per-agent metrics) that would catch an aggregate anomaly like §4's swollen recommendation queue; and if any stage executes autonomously rather than just recommending, is its blast radius capped to a volume a human could plausibly review if something went wrong, rather than running the full nightly batch as one uncontained unit. I'd also check the Ch15 delegation contract discipline specifically at each handoff — typed fields, not bare strings — because that's the fix for the join-problem failure mode this pipeline could equally suffer from, distinct from but just as damaging as a cascading defect. Finally, I'd ask what happens when one agent in the middle fails outright: does the pipeline checkpoint and surface a clean partial result, or does a mid-pipeline failure silently propagate a stale or default value downstream, which turns a clean failure into exactly the kind of hallucinated-tool-output mode §3 warns about, just relocated to an agent-to-agent boundary instead of a tool boundary.

**Q11: How do you decide whether a wrong-tool-selection problem should be fixed by rewriting the tool descriptions, adding an eval, or redesigning the tools themselves?**

Start with the eval, because you need the data before you can diagnose the cause — a labeled tool-selection test set (Ch17) run against the current tool set tells you whether the failure is occasional (a handful of genuinely ambiguous edge cases) or systematic (the model picks wrong on a whole class of inputs), and those two findings point to different fixes. Occasional, edge-case confusion is usually a description problem: the two tools' descriptions overlap in exactly the cases that confuse the model, and sharpening the language or adding a disambiguating example closes most of the gap cheaply. Systematic confusion across a whole input class is a stronger signal that the tools themselves are poorly separated at the design level — two tools that *should* be one tool with a parameter, or one tool that's actually trying to cover two genuinely different jobs — and no amount of description tuning fixes a design problem; that needs a tool redesign, the same way Ch13 argues tool schemas deserve API-design-level scrutiny, not prompt-level patching. The eval is what tells you which situation you're in before you spend engineering effort on the wrong fix.

**Q12: How does having this taxonomy change the conversation with a bank's risk committee or a regulator, compared to walking in with a list of past incidents?**

A list of past incidents is inherently backward-looking and inherently incomplete — it tells a regulator what already went wrong, says nothing about what hasn't yet, and invites the reasonable follow-up question of how confident anyone should be that the *next* incident won't be a brand-new failure mode nobody anticipated. A taxonomy, paired with the audit matrix from §8 and §9, reframes the conversation entirely: instead of seven stories, it's one framework — ten named failure modes, a named mitigation per mode, and for every agent in the MRM inventory (Ch20), a documented statement of which modes have been evaluated against and which haven't yet. That's a fundamentally different claim to defend: not "nothing has gone wrong that we know of," but "here is the complete space of ways this class of system fails, and here is exactly where our coverage currently stands, mode by mode, agent by agent." It also gives the regulator relationship somewhere productive to go in the next review cycle — the open gaps in the matrix become the agreed agenda for the next quarter's hardening work, rather than each new incident becoming its own unplanned, adversarial surprise.
