In an agentic platform, RAG is a *service the agent calls*, not the application itself. This chapter is about making the document leg excellent, because "agentic" doesn't fix bad retrieval — it just retries it, more expensively, and with more confidence in the wrong answer.

## 2. Why the industry needed it — chunking stopped being a preprocessing checkbox

Every team's first RAG pipeline treats chunking as a step you configure once and forget: pick a token count (512 is the folk-wisdom default), split, embed, move on. It works in the demo, because demo documents are short and demo questions don't land near a boundary. It keeps working in early production, because most real questions still don't land near a boundary. Then a question does — a clause split exactly where the chunker happened to cut, a conditional that lives in the *next* chunk over — and the system doesn't fail loudly. It answers confidently, cites a real document, and is quietly wrong, because nothing about a well-formed 512-token chunk *looks* truncated. §4 below is that failure, in full, because "chunking is a preprocessing detail" is the single most expensive assumption a RAG team makes, and it's almost always made silently, by whichever engineer configured the ingestion job first and never revisited it.

The industry's answer, matured over the last two years: chunking is a **design decision with its own trade-off space** (how a chunk is drawn) and its own **architectural timing decision** (when it's drawn — before indexing, or on demand at query time) — both decided per corpus, against an eval set, the same disciplined way Ch17 asks you to decide everything else.

## 3. A worked example — building the policy-document leg for the banking agent

Ch8's fee-waiver policy documents (the ones behind `check_policy`) are the running example. Walk through choosing a chunking strategy the way you'd actually do it, not by picking the first one you read about:

**Start with what the documents look like.** Bank policy documents are structured (numbered clauses, headers, cross-references), dense (a single sentence often carries a condition that changes the answer), and they change on a schedule (quarterly circulars, ad hoc amendments). That rules out the simplest option immediately: **fixed-size chunking** (split every N tokens, optionally with overlap) is fast and requires zero document understanding, but it cuts wherever the token count lands, with no awareness of a clause boundary — exactly the failure mode §4 walks through. It's the right default for unstructured, low-stakes text (meeting notes, short FAQs); it's the wrong default for anything a compliance answer will cite.

**Move to structure awareness.** **Document-based chunking** splits at the document's own boundaries — headers, numbered clauses, `<section>` tags — so a chunk boundary and a clause boundary are the same thing by construction. For policy documents this is the floor, not the ceiling: it guarantees you never split *within* a clause, but a single clause can still be too short to be useful alone (a cross-reference like "see clause 4.2" is meaningless without 4.2's content) or too long to embed precisely (a clause with five sub-conditions bundled together produces a noisy, averaged embedding that matches everything and nothing well).

**Add a second layer for precision and context together.** **Hierarchical chunking** keeps multiple granularities — the full policy, its sections, its individual clauses — so retrieval can match at the precise clause level while the system still has the surrounding section available to expand into. This is what "retrieve small, expand to parent" (the v1 version of this chapter's advice) actually means in practice: the small unit gives you retrieval precision, the parent gives the model context it needs to reason correctly, and the two are linked by metadata, not guessed at generation time.

**Consider the timing decision separately from the strategy.** All of the above is **pre-chunking**: documents are chunked once, offline, before any query arrives — fast at query time, but the chunking policy is baked into the index, so changing it means re-processing the corpus. The alternative, **post-chunking**, retrieves whole documents first and chunks them dynamically in response to the specific query — more flexible (the chunk boundary can be shaped by what the query actually needs) and more architecturally complex, at the cost of added latency on the first response. For a bank's policy corpus, where documents change on a predictable schedule and low query latency matters more than per-query chunking flexibility, pre-chunking is the right default — but this is exactly the kind of choice worth stating explicitly and revisiting with evidence, not assuming.

The resulting pipeline: document-based chunking as the structural floor, hierarchical linkage from clause to section to document, metadata on every chunk (document, section, clause number, effective date, product), pre-chunked and indexed offline, re-chunked only when a document version changes.

## 4. A failure story — the clause that got cut in half

Before the fee-waiver policy corpus moved to structure-aware chunking, it ran on the folk-wisdom default: fixed-size chunks of 512 tokens with a 50-token overlap. One clause read, in full: *"Waivers up to ₹2,000 are approved automatically for first-time disputes, provided the customer has no more than one prior waiver in the preceding 12 months."* The 512-token boundary fell — by pure accident of document length, nowhere near any clause the ingestion team had looked at — in the middle of that sentence, right before "provided." Chunk A ended with "...approved automatically for first-time disputes." Chunk B began with "provided the customer has no more than one prior waiver..." Neither chunk read as obviously broken: Chunk A is a complete, grammatically fine sentence on its own, and it's also exactly the kind of sentence a semantic-similarity search ranks highly against a query like "is this waiver approved automatically" — it's a strong, confident, on-topic match. Chunk B, starting mid-clause with "provided," carries weaker embedding signal for that same query and often didn't make the top-k cut at all.

For weeks, `check_policy` retrieved Chunk A, reasoned correctly over what it saw, and approved waivers with no visibility into the twelve-month condition living one chunk away. Nothing about any individual run looked wrong — the retrieved chunk was real, the citation was real, the model's reasoning over that chunk was sound. A compliance sample review, checking a batch of approved waivers against customers' full waiver history, found a cluster of repeat-waiver approvals that should have been declined. The root cause traced not to `check_policy`'s reasoning, not to the retrieval ranking, but to a chunk boundary drawn by a token counter that had never read the document it was cutting.

## 5. Design decisions

- **Chunking is chosen per corpus, against an eval set — never defaulted.** §3's decision tree (structure available? clauses need cross-linking? does timing matter more than flexibility?) is the reusable version of this; the wrong default, applied without an eval set to catch it, is exactly what produced §4's incident.
- **The chunk boundary and the citable unit should be the same thing.** If a compliance reviewer would cite "clause 4.2," your retrieval unit should be able to return exactly clause 4.2 — not an arbitrary 512-token window that happens to overlap it. This is the structural fix for §4: a document-based or hierarchical chunker cannot silently split a clause, because the clause boundary *is* the chunk boundary by construction.
- **Structure-aware beats fixed-size** for anything a citation depends on; fixed-size stays legitimate for low-stakes, unstructured text where speed matters more than precision (Ch8's context-budget bullets, meeting notes, FAQs).
- **Pre-chunk by default; post-chunk when the query genuinely needs to shape the boundary.** §3's timing decision — pre-chunking wins on latency and simplicity for a corpus that changes on a schedule; post-chunking earns its added complexity only when different queries need genuinely different chunk shapes from the same source document.
- **Metadata travels with the chunk, not beside it.** Document, section, clause number, effective date, product — attached at chunking time, filtered on at query time (§2b's `retrieve()`), and carried into the citation, so "which clause said that" is always answerable without a second lookup.

## 6. Trade-offs

- Bigger chunks: more context per hit, worse precision, costlier windows. Smaller: the reverse — and per §4, small-but-blind (fixed-size) trades precision you can't see you're losing until an incident finds it.
- Structure-aware and hierarchical chunking cost more engineering upfront (the chunker has to understand document structure, not just count tokens) — worth it exactly where §4's failure mode is expensive, i.e., anywhere a chunk becomes a compliance citation.
- Late chunking — embedding the full document first with a long-context model, then deriving chunk-level vectors from those pre-computed, context-aware tokens instead of embedding each chunk in isolation (the technique Jina AI published in 2024) — buys chunks that keep cross-references and pronouns resolvable against the whole document, at the cost of needing a long-context embedding model and more compute per document.
- Reranking adds ~100–300ms and a model dependency; almost always worth it above trivial corpora.
- Hybrid search doubles index maintenance; still the default because enterprise queries are id-heavy.
- Post-chunking's flexibility costs first-response latency and a more complex runtime path — don't pay for it on a corpus that doesn't need it.
- Multilingual (your Siddhi world): multilingual embedding models vs translate-then-embed — decide with evals per language pair, not vibes.

## 7. Industry implementation

**The chunking-strategy landscape has converged on a handful of named techniques**, worth knowing by name because they show up in vendor docs and interviews alike: fixed-size (fast, structure-blind), recursive (splits by a prioritized separator list — paragraphs, then sentences, then words — a solid default for unstructured text), document-based (splits at the document's own structural boundaries), semantic chunking (groups sentences by embedding similarity and cuts where the topic shifts, rather than by any fixed rule), LLM-based chunking (a model reads the document and proposes meaning-preserving boundaries directly), agentic chunking (an agent selects — or combines — whichever of the above fits a given document's structure and content), hierarchical chunking (multiple linked granularities, per §3), and late chunking (per §6, embed-the-whole-document-first). None of these is universally "best" — §3's decision process, not a leaderboard, is what picks one.

**The mature stack** is object store → parsing service → chunker → dual index (vector + keyword) → retrieval API with filters + reranker — exposed to agents *as a tool* with a clean schema (Ch13). Managed services (Bedrock Knowledge Bases, Azure AI Search, Vertex AI Search) give you the skeleton; the differentiating work — chunking policy, metadata schema, eval set, freshness pipeline — is never in the box, which is exactly why §4's incident is a chunking-policy failure and not a vendor failure: the vendor gave the team a chunker, not a chunking *decision*.

## 8. Hands-on lab

Build the policy-document leg for the banking agent, then prove the chunking choice with evidence rather than intuition:

**Stage 1 — build the eval set first.** 50 questions with known source clauses (include at least 10 where the correct answer depends on a conditional clause, mirroring §4). This eval set is what every later stage gets measured against — no chunking decision ships without a number from it.

**Stage 2 — reproduce §4 on purpose.** Ingest with plain fixed-size chunking (512 tokens, 50-token overlap) and run the eval set. Confirm you can reproduce the failure: at least one question whose correct answer depends on a conditional that a chunk boundary split away from its main clause.

**Stage 3 — fix it and re-measure.** Rebuild the index with document-based (or hierarchical) chunking, metadata-linked to section and clause. Re-run the identical eval set and report recall@5 and answer groundedness before and after — this before/after table, with the specific clause-split failure named, is the portfolio artifact.

**Stage 4 — the timing decision.** Argue, in writing, why this corpus is pre-chunked rather than post-chunked, referencing §3's decision criteria (document change cadence vs. query latency requirements) rather than asserting it.

## 9. Architect's take: the banking read

In a bank, RAG's differentiators are governance-shaped: retrieval must respect document classification and the *user's* entitlements (filter at query time by ACL, not by hoping the corpus is clean); citations must point to the authoritative version of the policy, because "the bot said X" incidents end with "which document version said X?"; and effective-dating is not optional when regulations change quarterly. §4 adds a specific, underrated fourth: the citation itself must be *complete* — a citation to a real, correctly-classified, current document version is still a liability if the retrieved chunk silently omits the clause's own condition. Bad RAG in a bank isn't just unhelpful — it's an incorrect-disclosure risk, and a chunking policy chosen without an eval set is how that risk gets introduced invisibly.

## Governance & security lens

RAG's core risk in an enterprise is *wrong or unauthorized disclosure*: retrieval must filter by the requesting user's entitlements at query time (never rely on a "clean" corpus), respect document classification, cite the authoritative version (incidents end with "which document version said that?"), and honor effective dates. The corpus is also an injection surface — a poisoned document is an instruction delivery vehicle, which is why retrieved content carries trust labels (Ch8). §4 adds a specific control worth naming: a chunking policy for any document that can be cited in a compliance decision should itself be reviewed and versioned, the same way the policy documents it chunks are — a silent token-count default is not a decision anyone signed off on. Governing questions: can a user ever retrieve a chunk their role couldn't read at the source, can we trace every generated claim to a governed document version, and can we show that our chunking policy never separates a clause from the conditions that qualify it?

## Interview Questions & Answers

**Q1: Why should chunking be treated as a design decision instead of a preprocessing step you configure once and move on from?**

Because the failure mode it produces doesn't announce itself — a badly chunked corpus doesn't throw errors, it just occasionally answers confidently and wrongly, citing a real document while omitting the part of the clause that changes the answer. That's exactly what happened on the fee-waiver corpus: fixed-size 512-token chunks with 50-token overlap ran for weeks looking completely healthy because every individual chunk was grammatically well-formed and every citation pointed to a real policy document. The problem only surfaced when a compliance sample review cross-checked approved waivers against customers' waiver history and found repeat approvals that should have been declined. Chunking decides what a citation *is* — the retrievable, citable unit of truth — so it has to be chosen per corpus against an eval set, the same disciplined way every other architectural trade-off in this course gets decided, not defaulted to whatever a tutorial used.

**Q2: Walk me through what happens when a chunk boundary lands in the middle of a clause, using a concrete example.**

Take the actual clause from the fee-waiver corpus: "Waivers up to ₹2,000 are approved automatically for first-time disputes, provided the customer has no more than one prior waiver in the preceding 12 months." Under fixed-size chunking, the 512-token boundary fell right before "provided," splitting it into two chunks — Chunk A, ending at "...first-time disputes," and Chunk B, starting mid-sentence with "provided the customer has no more than one prior waiver." Chunk A reads as a complete sentence and is exactly the kind of confident, on-topic text a similarity search ranks highly against a query like "is this waiver approved automatically," so it consistently won the top-k slot. Chunk B, starting awkwardly mid-clause, carried weaker embedding signal for that same query and often didn't make the cut at all — so the model reasoned soundly over a chunk that was, unknown to it, only half the rule.

**Q3: Once you detect and fix a chunking failure like the clause-split case, what has to happen downstream — what's the full consequence chain, not just the index rebuild?**

Fixing the index is the smallest part of the response. First, every decision `check_policy` made while running on the broken index needs to be identified and re-audited — in the real incident this meant compliance pulling the specific batch of approved waivers and checking them against customers' actual 12-month history, because some approvals were simply wrong and needed to be reversed or escalated. Second, the eval set itself needs a permanent addition covering conditional clauses like this one, so the same class of failure is caught by a regression test before it ever reaches production again. Third, the chunking policy change needs the same review and version control as the policy documents it's chunking, because a silent token-count default was never a decision anyone signed off on, and neither should its replacement be. The lesson generalizes: a retrieval bug in a regulated environment isn't closed by re-indexing, it's closed by tracing every decision made on the bad index back to a remediation.

**Q4: What are the actual cost trade-offs between chunking strategies, and where does reranking fit into that budget?**

Bigger chunks give you more context per retrieved hit but worse precision and a costlier context window per query; smaller chunks are the reverse, and per the clause-split incident, small-but-blind fixed-size chunking trades away precision you can't see you're losing until an incident finds it for you. Structure-aware and hierarchical chunking cost more upfront — the chunker has to understand document boundaries, not just count tokens — which is worth paying exactly where a chunk becomes a compliance citation, and not worth paying for a corpus like meeting notes. Late chunking, where you embed the full document with a long-context model first and then derive chunk-level vectors, buys cross-reference-aware chunks at the cost of needing a long-context embedding model and more compute per document. On top of the chunking choice, reranking adds roughly 100–300ms and a model dependency on every query, which is a real latency and infrastructure cost — but it's almost always worth paying above a trivial corpus size, because it's what turns a broad top-k candidate list into an actually-ranked answer.

**Q5: How do you make sure retrieval doesn't leak content a given user isn't entitled to see, especially once you've linked clauses to sections and full documents hierarchically?**

The rule is that entitlement filtering happens at query time, against the requesting user's actual ACL, never by assuming the corpus was curated cleanly beforehand — a user can retrieve a chunk their role couldn't read at the source if filtering is only applied upstream during ingestion. This matters more, not less, once you add hierarchical linkage: if retrieval can expand a matched clause up to its parent section or full document for context, that expansion path has to carry the same entitlement check as the initial retrieval, or the "expand to parent" mechanism becomes a way to smuggle a user into content they were correctly blocked from at the leaf level. Metadata that travels with the chunk — document, section, clause, classification — is what makes that filter enforceable in the first place, since you can't filter by entitlement on a chunk that doesn't carry the classification to check against. The governing question worth asking in review is literally that: can a user, through any retrieval or expansion path, ever end up seeing a chunk their role couldn't read at the source.

**Q6: What guardrails stop the system from generating a confident answer when the retrieved chunks don't actually cover the question?**

The floor is that the system has to be able to say "not in sources" — an enterprise RAG system that can't do that isn't a retrieval system, it's a liability generator, because the alternative is the model filling the gap with plausible-sounding text that has no citation behind it. That guardrail has to be paired with the structural fix from the clause-split incident: even when a citation exists, it isn't sufficient on its own, because a real, correctly classified, current citation can still be incomplete if the chunk it points to silently omits the clause's qualifying condition. So the practical control is two-layered — refuse to answer past the edge of what was retrieved, and make the retrieval unit itself match the citable unit (clause-level, not an arbitrary token window) so that when a citation is offered, it's actually complete. Reranking and a groundedness eval — checking that generated claims trace back to the specific document version cited — are what make "not in sources" enforceable rather than aspirational.

**Q7: Where exactly does access control get enforced in a RAG pipeline — index build time, or query time — and why does that choice matter?**

It has to be enforced at query time, filtering by the requesting user's live entitlements against each chunk's classification metadata, rather than relying on the index having been built from an already-clean, pre-filtered corpus. The reason is that entitlements and document classifications both change independently of the index — a role's access can be revoked, or a document's classification tightened, between indexing runs — and a query-time filter is the only point that's guaranteed to reflect the current state rather than whatever was true when the corpus was last chunked and embedded. This is also why metadata has to travel with the chunk from the moment it's created rather than being attached separately later: a retrieval API can only filter on entitlement, classification, and effective date if that information is present on every candidate chunk before ranking happens, not bolted on afterward. Practically, this makes the retrieval API's filter layer as load-bearing as the chunking policy itself — it's the last-mile check between a broad candidate set and what the user is actually allowed to see.

**Q8: How do you handle index freshness in production when the underlying documents — like quarterly policy circulars — change on a schedule?**

Because policy documents are pre-chunked (built once, offline, before any query arrives) for latency and simplicity reasons, freshness has to be managed as an explicit re-chunking trigger rather than assumed to happen automatically: a document version change re-chunks and re-indexes just that document, with clause- and section-level metadata updated to reflect the new effective date. This is exactly why effective-dating on every chunk isn't optional — if two versions of a clause can coexist in an index during a rollout window, the system needs to know which one is currently authoritative so it never cites a superseded rule, especially given how often bank policy changes quarterly or ad hoc. Reindexing cadence should be driven by the corpus's actual change cadence rather than a fixed calendar job, because a policy that updates mid-quarter and waits for a scheduled reindex is a live-in-production incorrect-disclosure risk in exactly the same shape as the clause-split failure, just caused by staleness instead of a bad boundary. The operational discipline that makes this auditable is the same one that makes citations trustworthy: every generated claim has to be traceable to a specific, governed document version, not just "the policy corpus" in the abstract.

**Q9: A bank wants to launch a policy-lookup assistant over its fee-waiver documents. Design the retrieval leg end to end.**

Start from what the documents actually look like — structured, numbered clauses with cross-references, dense enough that a single sentence can carry a condition that changes the answer, and changing on a predictable quarterly schedule — which rules out fixed-size chunking immediately given what it did to the ₹2,000 waiver clause. Use document-based chunking as the structural floor so a chunk boundary is always a clause boundary by construction, add a hierarchical layer linking clause to section to document so retrieval can match precisely at the clause level while still being able to expand to surrounding context, and attach metadata — document, section, clause number, effective date, product, classification — to every chunk at chunking time rather than bolting it on later. Pre-chunk offline given the predictable change cadence, index with both vector and keyword search since compliance queries are often identifier-heavy ("clause 4.2," specific product codes), add a reranker to turn the broad candidate set into an actually-ordered result, and filter every query by the requesting user's live entitlements before anything reaches the reranker. Before any of this ships, build a 50-question eval set with known source clauses — including several where the correct answer depends on a conditional, deliberately mirroring the clause-split failure — and require a recall@5 and groundedness number from that eval set before and after any chunking change, so the launch decision is made on evidence rather than intuition.

**Q10: (Common phrasing: "How would you design a production RAG system?") What does "production-ready" actually add on top of a working retrieval demo?**

A demo RAG pipeline usually stops at embed-chunks-and-retrieve-top-k, and that's the part that keeps working right up until a question lands near a chunk boundary — which is precisely why "agentic" or "production" doesn't fix bad retrieval, it just retries a wrong answer more expensively and with more apparent confidence. Production-ready means the retrieval leg is a governed service, not a script: chunking chosen per corpus against a real eval set, entitlement filtering enforced at query time rather than assumed from a clean corpus, citations that point to an authoritative, effective-dated document version, and a reranker sitting between broad recall and what the model actually sees. It also means the pipeline is measured continuously rather than validated once — recall@5 and groundedness tracked against the eval set as the corpus and chunking policy evolve, not just checked at initial launch. The managed pieces — object store, parsing, dual index, retrieval API — are what a vendor like Bedrock Knowledge Bases or Azure AI Search gives you for free; the chunking policy, metadata schema, eval set, and freshness pipeline are never in that box, and that's exactly the differentiating work "production-ready" refers to.

**Q11: (Common phrasing: "How do you evaluate and compare chunking strategies?") What does that evaluation actually look like in practice, rather than in theory?**

It starts with a purpose-built eval set of real questions with known source clauses — fifty is a workable number for a mid-sized policy corpus — deliberately including a chunk of questions, at least ten in this case, whose correct answer depends on a conditional clause, because that's the exact failure class a naive fixed-size chunker misses. You then reproduce the baseline on purpose: ingest with plain fixed-size chunking and confirm the eval set actually surfaces at least one case where a conditional got split away from its main clause, so you know your eval set is sensitive enough to catch the failure you're trying to prevent. Rebuild the index with the candidate strategy — document-based or hierarchical, metadata-linked to section and clause — and re-run the identical eval set, reporting recall@5 and answer groundedness before and after as a single comparison table. That before/after table, with the specific clause-split failure named rather than a generic score, is what makes the chunking decision defensible in a review — it shows the choice was made on evidence, not on whichever strategy read best in a blog post.

**Q12: (Common phrasing: "Why use hybrid search instead of pure vector similarity?") What does hybrid search actually buy you in an enterprise setting that pure embeddings don't?**

Pure vector similarity is excellent at matching meaning and paraphrase, but enterprise queries in a bank are frequently full of exact identifiers — a clause number, a product code, a customer reference — and semantic similarity search is notoriously weak at guaranteeing an exact-string match ranks where it should. Hybrid search pairs a vector index with a keyword index precisely to cover that gap, so a query for "clause 4.2" or a specific product code hits the right chunk deterministically instead of depending on embedding proximity to carry an identifier match. The cost is real — hybrid search roughly doubles index maintenance, since you're building and keeping two indexes in sync rather than one — but it's still the right default for enterprise corpora because the failure mode of missing an exact identifier match is common and costly enough to justify it. It's also complementary to the reranker rather than a substitute for one: hybrid search widens and improves the candidate set that gets generated, reranking is what orders that combined candidate set correctly before it reaches the model.
